How to Become a Hypnotherapist in Ireland

John A O'Connor, Clinical Hypnotherapist 12 September 2026 4 minute read

There is no single, official route to becoming a hypnotherapist in Ireland. That is the first thing worth understanding, because it shapes every other decision: which course to take, which body to join, and how clients will judge whether you are properly qualified.

This guide sets out what the path usually involves, what to check along the way, and some honest realities about the work.

Is hypnotherapy regulated in Ireland?

Not by statute. CORU, Ireland's regulator for health and social care professionals, regulates professions designated under the Health and Social Care Professionals Act 2005. Counsellors and psychotherapists were designated in 2019, and CORU's registers for psychologists are still being established. Hypnotherapy is not a designated profession.

In practice, that means the title "hypnotherapist" is not legally protected. Anyone can use it. The absence of statutory regulation puts more weight on three things you choose yourself: the quality of your training, the professional body you belong to, and your insurance.

It also cuts the other way. Because clients cannot rely on a state register, a growing number check for accreditation before they book. Doing this properly is what separates a practice people trust from one they do not.

Step 1: Be honest about why

People come to this work for good reasons: a personal experience of change, a background in teaching or care, or a long fascination with how the mind works. It is worth also being clear about the realities.

  • Most hypnotherapists are self-employed, and a practice usually takes time to build.
  • Clients bring difficult material. The work can be emotionally demanding.
  • You will need to know your limits and refer people on when something is beyond your scope.

Step 2: Choose training carefully

Courses vary enormously, from weekend certificates to structured diplomas with supervised practice. Before enrolling, ask any provider:

  • How many hours of teaching, and how much of it is live rather than recorded?
  • Is there supervised practice with real clients, and is casework assessed?
  • Does the course cover ethics, safeguarding, and when to refer someone to a GP?
  • Which professional body recognises the qualification, and will it make you eligible for membership?
  • Who teaches it, and what is their own clinical experience?

If a provider cannot answer those questions clearly, that is an answer in itself.

Step 3: Join a professional body

Membership of a professional body is the main form of accountability available in an unregulated field. A credible body will have a code of ethics, a complaints procedure, continuing professional development requirements, and a public directory of members.

The European Association of Professional Hypnotherapists (EAPH), established in 1996, is one such body in Ireland. It accepts graduates of the Institute of Clinical Hypnotherapy & Psychotherapy or therapists with equivalent training, and lists current members in its directory. Check membership criteria directly with any body you are considering, as they can change.

Step 4: Get insured

Professional indemnity and public liability insurance are basic requirements. Many professional bodies require proof of cover for membership, and venues that rent therapy rooms often ask for it too.

Step 5: Understand the legal and ethical basics

  • Scope of practice. Hypnotherapy is not a substitute for medical or psychiatric care. Knowing when to recommend a GP is part of the job.
  • Data protection. Client notes include health information, which is special category data under GDPR and needs careful handling and a clear privacy policy.
  • Advertising. Claims about health outcomes are covered by the ASAI Code. Avoid promising results.

Step 6: Keep learning

Qualification is the start. Continuing professional development and supervision, meaning regular reflective discussion of your casework with an experienced practitioner, keep a practice safe and effective over time.

A second career is common

Many hypnotherapists come to the work after another career, and that background often turns out to be an asset.

John A O'Connor is an example. As he described on the EAPH's Professional Hypnotherapists Podcast, he worked as a mechanic and engineer, and later lectured in engineering at college level, before becoming a hypnotherapist. His interest went back much further, to meeting the stage hypnotist Paul Goldin as a seven year old.

Teaching, in particular, transfers well. Explaining something clearly, noticing when someone has not understood, and adjusting to the person in front of you are much of what good therapy involves. So is life experience, including experience of your own setbacks.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a psychology degree to become a hypnotherapist?
There is no legal requirement, because hypnotherapy is not statutorily regulated in Ireland. Individual courses and professional bodies set their own entry and membership criteria.
How long does hypnotherapy training take?
It varies widely, from short certificates to diplomas with supervised practice over a longer period. Be cautious of very short courses that promise full qualification.
Is hypnotherapy regulated in Ireland?
No. CORU regulates designated health and social care professions, and hypnotherapy is not one of them. Professional body membership and insurance are the main forms of accountability.
Can I train online?
Some teaching can be done online, but look for courses that include live, supervised practice with real clients rather than recorded content alone.
Can I practise alongside another job?
Many people start part-time while building a client base. Insurance, professional body membership and proper record-keeping still apply from the first client.
