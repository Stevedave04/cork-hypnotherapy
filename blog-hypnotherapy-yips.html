The Yips: Why Golfers, GAA Free-Takers and Rugby Kickers Lose It Under Pressure

John A O'Connor, Clinical Hypnotherapist 22 September 2026 4 minute read

A club golfer with a smooth, practised putting stroke stands over a two foot putt and cannot bring the club back. A GAA forward who has scored a hundred frees suddenly cannot trust the strike. A goal-kicker who used to be automatic starts second-guessing the routine mid-swing.

Coaches call it the yips. It looks like a technical breakdown, and the instinct is to fix it with more technique: more reps, more coaching, more video analysis of the stroke. For most people who actually have the yips, that makes very little difference, because the problem was never in the technique.

What the yips actually are

The yips are a disruption to a skill that used to be automatic. The defining feature is that the skill still works fine in low-stakes conditions: on the range, in the warm-up, in a friendly. It breaks down specifically when it starts to matter, which is the signature of a fear response rather than a physical or technical problem.

Once a skilled, overlearned movement gets consciously monitored under pressure, in sports psychology sometimes called "paralysis by analysis," the automatic pattern is disrupted by the attempt to control it. A single bad miss at a costly moment is often enough to start the cycle: the fear of missing again causes exactly the kind of conscious interference that produces another miss.

From there it compounds. Confidence in the skill drops, the checking and second-guessing increases, and the action that used to be effortless becomes the most anxious moment of the game.

Why more practice usually does not fix it

Practising the technique again assumes the technique is broken. For most yips cases it is not. The stroke that goes wrong under pressure is usually identical, mechanically, to the one that works fine in practice. Grinding the same drill does not touch the actual problem, which is what happens to that skill under threat.

This is why players can describe knowing exactly what they are doing wrong, and still be unable to stop doing it. Knowing is not the level the problem sits at.

Where hypnotherapy fits

The work targets the automatic threat response that gets triggered the moment the situation matters: the tightening, the sudden conscious monitoring, the anticipation of missing before the action has even started. Reducing that response is what lets the pre-existing, already-learned skill run the way it used to, without the interference.

Part of the work is also rehearsal: going through the exact moment, the stance, the routine, the strike, under hypnosis, calmly and repeatedly, so the nervous system has a different, successful version of that specific moment to draw on rather than only the recent misses.

This is not a replacement for coaching, and a player whose technique genuinely has broken down still needs a coach to look at it. The two are often confused, which is part of why the yips get treated as a technical problem for far longer than they should.

What it will not do

It will not build a skill that was never there. If the underlying technique is genuinely faulty, that is coaching work, not hypnotherapy. And it is not a one-session fix before a big match; the same as any performance work, it settles better with a few weeks in hand.

The yips are a well-documented phenomenon in sports psychology, not a mysterious or shameful one. Some of the most accomplished players in golf and other precision sports have gone through it publicly.

Frequently asked questions

Are the yips physical or psychological?
Overwhelmingly psychological, even though the result looks physical. The stroke usually works fine outside pressure situations, which points to a fear response rather than a mechanical fault.
Can hypnotherapy fix bad technique?
No, and it should not be used to try. If a coach identifies a genuine technical fault, that needs coaching. Hypnotherapy is for skills that work fine in practice but break down specifically under pressure.
How many sessions does this usually take?
Often similar to other phobia-type work, in the region of two to four sessions, though it depends on how long the pattern has been established and how the individual responds.
Does this work for team sports as well as golf?
Yes. Free-taking, penalty-taking, and any repeated pressure skill in GAA, rugby or other sports responds to the same underlying approach.
Can this be done as part of work with a club?
Yes, either as individual sessions for an affected player or alongside the broader mental performance work covered on the sports page.
