Visualisation in Sport: What Hypnotherapy Actually Adds

John A O'Connor, Clinical Hypnotherapist 24 September 2026 3 minute read

Visualisation has been part of elite sport for a long time. Jack Nicklaus wrote that he never hit a shot, even in practice, without first running a sharp, in-focus picture of it in his mind. Seve Ballesteros was well known for the same habit, rehearsing a round in detail before he played a stroke.

Most players have tried some version of it: picturing the free landing, the putt dropping, the tackle going in clean. For a lot of people it stays a vague good intention rather than something that reliably changes how they perform. The gap between the two is usually structure.

What the research actually supports

Mental rehearsal engages some of the same neural pathways as physically performing the action, which is part of why athletes who combine visualisation with physical practice tend to outperform those who rely on physical practice alone. It is not a replacement for reps. It is a way of adding rehearsal time without extra physical load, and of rehearsing the state a player wants to be in as well as the movement itself.

The quality of the visualisation matters more than how often it is done. Vague, passive imagining, a fuzzy picture of "playing well," adds very little. Detailed, first-person, multi-sensory rehearsal, seeing it through your own eyes, feeling the weight of the ball, hearing the crowd, adds considerably more.

Where the structured version differs

Most players' own visualisation happens in an ordinary, distracted state of mind: a few seconds in the car, half-focused the night before a match. It works, but it is a fairly shallow version of the tool.

Under hypnosis, the same rehearsal happens in a state of much deeper focus, with far less of the mental noise that normally competes for attention. The picture built is deliberately vivid and specific: the exact routine, the exact conditions, the exact feeling of execution going well, repeated until it is genuinely familiar rather than aspirational.

This also allows something ordinary visualisation rarely does well: rehearsing composure under a specific pressure moment, not just the technical action. A player can rehearse not just the free-kick itself but the walk up to it with a settled mind, which is often the part that actually breaks down in a match.

How it is used with a club or an individual

The starting point is usually the specific moment that matters most: a pre-match routine, a set-piece, the first five minutes after a mistake. Rather than a generic "visualise success" script, the rehearsal is built around what that player or team actually does, so it transfers directly onto the pitch or course rather than staying an abstract exercise.

Once a player has been through the process a few times, the same routine can often be self-directed before matches, which is the aim: a reliable mental warm-up they can use without needing a session every week.

What it will not do

Visualisation, however well structured, does not substitute for physical practice, coaching, or fitness. It sharpens what is already trained. A skill that has never been built physically will not appear from rehearsal alone.

Frequently asked questions

Is visualisation just imagining winning?
No, and that vaguer version is largely why it does not work well for a lot of people. Effective visualisation is specific and multi-sensory: the exact routine, conditions, and feeling of good execution, rehearsed in detail.
How is this different to what I already do before a game?
Most players' own visualisation happens in an ordinary, distracted state. Doing it under hypnosis allows a much deeper, more focused version, with less mental noise competing for attention.
Does visualisation actually improve performance, or is it just confidence?
Both, to some extent. It appears to engage some of the same mental processes as physically performing the action, which is part of why it is used alongside, not instead of, physical practice.
Can I learn to do this myself afterwards?
Usually, yes. Once the routine has been rehearsed a few times in session, most people can use a shortened version themselves before matches.
Is this only useful before a big event, or an ongoing thing?
It works best built into regular preparation rather than used once before a single match, in the same way physical training is ongoing rather than a one-off.
